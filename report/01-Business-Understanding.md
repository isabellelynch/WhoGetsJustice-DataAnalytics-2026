## 1.1 Context and Problem

In the United States, a substantial proportion of homicides are never cleared. An analysis of FBI Uniform Crime Reports
(UCR) and Supplementary Homicide Reports (SHR) by The Murder Accountability Project found that 230,355 of the 638,228 
homicides reported between 1980 and 2014, approximately 36.1%, remain unresolved [1]. Under UCR guidelines, an offence 
is considered cleared “in one of two ways: by arrest or by exceptional means” [2]. An uncleared homicide is therefore 
one in which no offender has been identified and charged, and no exceptional circumstances apply. 

The consequences of non-clearance are significant. Victims’ families are left without resolution, 
offenders remain at large with the potential to reoffend, and public confidence in policing is weakened [3]. There is 
also evidence that clearance outcomes are not evenly distributed, differing across weapon types, jurisdictions and 
victim demographics [4]. Since investigative resources are finite and early investigative activity is widely regarded 
as critical to case outcomes [3], an early indication of which cases are at elevated risk of going unsolved could help 
supervisors and oversight bodies allocate attention more effectively. 

## 1.2 Stakeholders and Users

This data is collected by local police agencies and accessed via the FBI Supplementary Homicide Reports, for the purpose 
of this report a dataset created by the Murder Accountability Project will be used. This dataset compiles all Homicide 
Reports from 1980 to 2014 into one dataset. Reporting is voluntary, therefore data quality varies by agency. 

The results of this analysis are intended to be used primarily by homicide-unit supervisors and police oversight bodies 
to help them identify where the system falls and if resources can be better spent in areas most affected. Their primary 
objective is to spot and reduce gaps in which cases get solved. The results may also be of use to criminology researchers 
and policymakers, who want to understand systematic patterns. 

Those most affected by this data are the victims’ families and communities with lower clearance rates. This data includes 
a lot of missing data and meaningless codes which can affect how a Model may interpret the data and often leads to the 
Model copying existing inequalities.
